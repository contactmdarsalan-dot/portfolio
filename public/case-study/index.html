Project Overview

Building Nepal's Most Intuitive HR Software from the Ground Up

Hajir Khata is a cloud-based attendance and payroll SaaS platform built for Nepali businesses. As Lead Product Designer, I owned the entire design lifecycle — user research, information architecture, wireframes, high-fidelity UI, design system, and developer handoff — across both web and mobile platforms.

The word "Hajir" means present in Nepali — the name itself is the product promise: making employee management effortlessly visible, accurate, and always accessible.

Design Process

From Research to Launch — A Human-Centered Process

I followed a Double Diamond approach across six structured phases, from discovery research through final developer handoff and implementation support.

01

Discover

Interviews, field observation, competitive audit

02

Define

Personas, journey maps, problem statements

03

Ideate

IA, wireframes, design sprints

04

Prototype

High-fidelity Figma prototypes

05

Test

Usability testing, 3 rounds of iteration

06

Deliver

Design system, dev handoff, QA

Phase 1 · Weeks 1–3

Discovery & Research

18 stakeholder interviews, contextual observation, affinity mapping, 3 primary user personas. Competitive analysis of Nimble HRMS, e-HR Online, Keka, ZohoHR.

Phase 2 · Weeks 4–5

Information Architecture & Flows

Full IA for both web portal and mobile app. User flow diagrams for all critical paths: onboarding, attendance, leave requests, payroll, and report generation.

Phase 3 · Weeks 6–10

Wireframing & Low-Fidelity Prototyping

120+ wireframe screens for web dashboard, mobile app, and admin portal. Two rounds of wireframe testing with HR professionals to validate navigation logic and layout hierarchy.

Phase 4 · Weeks 11–18

Visual Design & Design System

Full design system in Figma: color tokens, typography scale, 60+ components, icon set, responsive grid. High-fidelity UI applied to all 120+ screens on both platforms.

Phase 5 · Weeks 19–22

Prototyping, Testing & Iteration

Interactive Figma prototypes for all major flows. 3 rounds of moderated usability testing with 12 participants. 28 usability issues identified and resolved.

Phase 6 · Weeks 23–26

Developer Handoff & Launch Support

Detailed handoff documentation, design QA during development, pixel-accurate implementation checks, and post-launch iteration based on real user feedback.

Design Decisions
01

Bikram Sambat calendar

Nepali businesses use BS dates for all official records. Showing BS date on the dashboard immediately signals this is built for Nepal.

02

4 KPIs, not 8

Early wireframe had 8 stat cards. Usability testing showed users only needed Present, Absent, On Leave, Total — everything else moved to drill-down pages.

03

Icon-only collapsed sidebar

Most users spend 70% of time on the dashboard. Keeping the sidebar collapsed by default gives more screen space to the data that matters.

04

Activity feed on the right

HR managers told us they check "who came in late?" first thing in the morning. The activity feed answers this without any navigation.

01

Color-coded status badges

HR managers scan this table quickly. Color coding (green/red/orange/blue) lets them spot problems in under 2 seconds without reading every row.

Photos require extra management overhead. Initials with colored circles provide quick visual identification without the maintenance burden.

In Nepal's labour context, "late" and "absent" have different payroll implications. Separating them in the badge system was critical for accurate record-keeping.

Design Decisions
01

Nepali rupee symbol (रू)

Using the correct currency symbol (not Rs. or NRS) was a deliberate trust signal — telling users this product was made for them, not adapted from abroad.

02

Earnings in green, deductions in red

Immediate visual language for what adds vs. removes from salary. Even a first-time user understands the breakdown without reading labels carefully.

03

Net pay is the hero number

Employees care most about "how much do I get?" We made net pay the largest, boldest number on the screen — not buried in a table row.

04

PF and TDS built in by default

In early wireframes these were optional fields. Research showed 100% of formal Nepali businesses need these — so they became required default line items.

Design Decisions
01

One big button, nothing else

Usability testing showed that non-tech-savvy field workers struggled with screens that had multiple actions. The check-in button is the only major interactive element — impossible to miss.

02

GPS shown, not hidden

Employees were suspicious of location tracking. Showing GPS status explicitly ("Location Verified, New Baneshwor") built trust — they know it's working, not watching.

03

Self-awareness about lateness

Showing the employee their own "Late" status before an HR manager flags it reduces conflict — employees self-correct when they have visibility.

Design Decisions
01

Balance cards first, not last

Original wireframes had the balance summary at the bottom. Testing showed managers wanted to know "how many people are on leave today" before anything else — so balances moved to the top.

02

"+ Request" embedded in balance row

Employees spent 3+ clicks to find the leave request form. Embedding it in the balance card row reduced task completion time by 60% in usability testing.

03

Nepali dates throughout (Bai = Baisakh)

All leave records use Bikram Sambat dates. HR managers work in BS natively — showing AD dates forced them to mentally convert, adding cognitive load.

Design Principles

The UX Principles Behind Every Design Decision

01

Complexity hidden, clarity shown

Payroll is complex under the hood — the interface must feel effortlessly simple.

02

Mobile-first for the field

Nepal has a large field workforce. Mobile experience is primary, not secondary.

03

Nepal-native by default

Nepali calendar (BS), local tax laws, familiar payment terminology — built in, not bolted on.

04

Trust through transparency

Employees must understand exactly how their salary is calculated. Every deduction must be visible and labeled.

05

Zero training required

An HR manager in a small shop should be able to use this on day one without any training materials.

Design System

A Scalable System for 120+ Screens

I built a complete Figma design system that enabled visual consistency across all screens and allowed rapid future development without design drift.

🎨

Color tokens

Brand green, semantic states (success, warning, danger, info), and a neutral gray scale — all as Figma tokens for easy theming.

🔤

Typography scale

6-step scale: display, heading, body, label, caption, overline — consistent across web and mobile platforms.

🧩

Component library

60+ reusable components: buttons, inputs, tables, badges, modals, navigation, empty states — with all variant states.

📐

Responsive grid

12-column grid for web (3 breakpoints) and an 8-point grid system for mobile — applied consistently across all 120+ screens.

Reflections

What This Project Taught Me as a Product Designer

🌏

Context is everything

Designing for Nepal required deep local knowledge. Generic assumptions would have failed users at every turn — the calendar, the currency symbol, the tax structure.

🔬

Research saves rework

Every hour in Phase 1 research prevented days of rework later. The payslip net-pay decision alone saved weeks of redesign after discovering real user behavior.

🧱

Systems thinking pays off

The design system felt slow to build at first but made every subsequent screen faster, more consistent, and dramatically easier to hand off to developers.

🤝

Designers must speak business

Framing design decisions in terms of outcomes (adoption, support cost reduction, NPS) was key to getting stakeholder alignment on every major design decision.

The best product design is invisible. Users shouldn't notice the design — they should simply feel like the product understands them. With Hajir Khata, the goal was to make Nepal's hardest HR admin task feel as easy as checking your phone. The market reception suggests we got there.
